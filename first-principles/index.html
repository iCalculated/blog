binary search

00 / divide & conquer

Think of guessing a number between 1 and 100, told "higher" or "lower" each time. A sorted list lets you find anything the same way—by halving. Guess the middle item; the target is either there, to the left, or to the right, so you throw away half of what's left every guess. The number of guesses you need is just how many times you can halve the list: about 30 for a billion items. Pick a target and step through it.

stacks

01 / last in, first out

A stack is a pile of plates. You add to the top (push) and you take from the top (pop)—never the middle. LIFO, for "last in, first out": the last thing you put in is the first thing back out. It's how undo works, how a browser's back button works, and—under the hood—how a program keeps its place when one task interrupts another.

queues

02 / first in, first out

A queue is the line at a counter. You join at the back (called enqueue) and you're served from the front (dequeue). FIFO, "first in, first out": fairness by arrival order. It's how a printer handles jobs in the order you sent them, and how a help desk serves tickets first-come, first-served.

hash tables

03 / instant lookup

How do you find a value instantly, without searching at all? You calculate exactly where it lives. A hash function turns a key (like a word) into a bucket number, so storing and finding skip searching entirely. Here, every letter has a number; add them up, take the remainder after dividing by 8, and that picks one of 8 buckets. When two keys land in the same bucket (a collision), you simply keep both, side by side. This is the dictionary / map / lookup behind nearly every language.

sorting

04 / order from chaos

Putting things in order is the most-studied problem in computing—so much else depends on it (binary search needs a sorted list to begin with!). The naive ways compare everything to everything: simple, but they get dramatically slower as the list grows. The clever ones split the list in half over and over—the same halving trick from binary search—and stay fast. Watch the difference: cyan compares, red swaps, green is settled. how things scale

05 / big-o notation

Now the idea that ties the rest together. Say you double the size of a problem—does the work double too? Quadruple? Barely change? That single question decides what's possible at scale, and big-O is the shorthand for the answer. Read n as "the number of items." O(1) is flat work, no matter the size. O(n) means twice the data, twice the work. O(n²) means twice the data, four times the work—a cliff. O(log n)—binary search's halving—barely stirs. Drag the slider and watch them part ways.
